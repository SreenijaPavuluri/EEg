\title{Riemannian Geometry vs. Neural Baselines for Cross-Subject\\
Motor Imagery EEG: A Calibration-Aware Benchmark}

\begin{abstract}
Foundation models for electroencephalography (EEG) have shown promising
results on motor imagery (MI) classification, yet two critical gaps remain
largely unaddressed: (1) systematic comparison with Riemannian geometry baselines,
and (2) evaluation of prediction \emph{calibration}—the agreement between
model confidence and empirical accuracy. We present the first benchmark that
directly addresses both gaps under strict subject-independent leave-one-subject-out
(LOSO) evaluation. Using the publicly available PhysioNet EEG Motor
Movement/Imagery Database (EEGMMIDB, 30 subjects, 64 channels), we evaluate
six representative methods spanning classical feature engineering
(log-variance + LDA, filter-bank CSP + LDA), Riemannian geometry
(Minimum Distance to Mean, tangent-space SVM), and compact neural architectures
(EEGNet, ShallowConvNet). Beyond standard accuracy, balanced accuracy, Cohen's
$\kappa$, and AUROC, we report Expected Calibration Error (ECE) and Brier score
for every method. Our results reveal that Riemannian methods are competitive with
neural baselines on cross-subject transfer while producing better-calibrated
probability outputs—a property critical for clinical deployment. All code,
data pipelines, and results are publicly available.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Motor imagery (MI) EEG classification is a cornerstone paradigm in
brain-computer interfaces (BCIs), enabling non-invasive communication and
motor rehabilitation. A subject imagines moving a body part (e.g., left or right
hand), and a classifier decodes the corresponding EEG patterns. Despite decades
of progress, cross-subject generalization—where a model trained on one set of
subjects is applied to a new, unseen subject—remains the primary practical
bottleneck \cite{lotte2018review}.

Recent EEG foundation models, notably MIRepNet \cite{mireNet2025} and the general
EEG representation models such as LaBraM \cite{labram2024}, EEGPT
\cite{eegpt2024}, and CBraMod \cite{cbramod2025}, have pushed MI classification
performance by pre-training on large multi-dataset corpora. The LEAD framework
\cite{lead2025} similarly demonstrates large-scale EEG pre-training for
Alzheimer's detection with subject-independent evaluation. However, we identify
two critical gaps in this growing literature:

\textbf{Gap 1—Riemannian baselines are systematically omitted.} Riemannian
geometry on the symmetric positive definite (SPD) manifold of EEG covariance
matrices has established itself as one of the strongest classical approaches
for MI BCI \cite{barachant2012multiclass, congedo2017riemannian}. Yet
MIRepNet, LEAD, and related foundation models do not include Riemannian
methods in their comparisons, making it impossible to assess how much the
added model complexity of a foundation model actually contributes relative
to this strong baseline.

\textbf{Gap 2—Calibration of EEG classifiers is never reported.} Expected
calibration error (ECE) and Brier score measure the agreement between predicted
class probabilities and empirical outcomes. Miscalibrated models may achieve
high accuracy while producing unreliable confidence scores—a serious concern for
clinical BCI deployment. None of the reviewed EEG foundation model papers report
any calibration metric.

We address both gaps with a controlled, reproducible benchmark on a single large
public dataset, designed to be fully reproducible on a standard CPU laptop. Our
contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item The first calibration-aware cross-subject MI benchmark on PhysioNet
  EEGMMIDB, reporting ECE and Brier score alongside standard MI metrics.
\item The first direct head-to-head comparison of Riemannian geometry methods
  vs.\ compact neural architectures under strict LOSO evaluation.
\item A publicly released codebase that is CPU-feasible and fully reproducible.
\item An analysis of the accuracy–calibration tradeoff across method families,
  with implications for EEG foundation model evaluation.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{EEG Foundation Models}

A recent wave of EEG foundation models applies transformer-based or Mamba-based
self-supervised pre-training to large EEG corpora. LaBraM \cite{labram2024} and
LaBraM++ \cite{labramplusplus2025} use codebook-based transformers; EEGPT
\cite{eegpt2024} applies GPT-style pre-training; CBraMod \cite{cbramod2025}
introduces a criss-cross attention mechanism; LUNA \cite{luna2025} and
DIVER-0 \cite{diver2025} target topology-agnostic or equivariant representations.
Task-specific models include MIRepNet \cite{mireNet2025}, which applies hybrid
pre-training specifically for MI tasks on five public datasets, and LEAD
\cite{lead2025}, which addresses EEG-based Alzheimer's detection.

Despite impressive accuracy numbers, these papers share a common limitation: they
benchmark primarily against other neural baselines and rarely include the
Riemannian geometry approaches that have been SOTA in MI BCI for over a decade.

\subsection{Riemannian Geometry for MI BCI}

Barachant et al.\ \cite{barachant2012multiclass} introduced Riemannian distance
measures on SPD manifolds for EEG classification, later formalized into the
Minimum Distance to Mean (MDM) classifier \cite{barachant2013riemannian}. The
tangent-space projection maps SPD matrices to a Euclidean space where standard
classifiers (SVM, LDA) can be applied \cite{congedo2017riemannian}. Riemannian
methods won or placed highly in multiple BCI Competition editions and remain
strong baselines \cite{lotte2018review}.

\subsection{Calibration in Machine Learning}

Calibration measures how well a model's output probabilities reflect empirical
frequencies \cite{niculescu2005predicting, guo2017calibration}. Expected
calibration error (ECE) bins predictions by confidence and measures the
weighted average absolute deviation from perfect calibration. Brier score
measures mean squared probability error. Temperature scaling \cite{guo2017calibration}
and isotonic regression \cite{niculescu2005predicting} are common post-hoc
calibration methods. Calibration has been studied extensively in medical
AI \cite{vaicenavicius2019evaluating} but is largely absent from the BCI literature.

\subsection{Filter-Bank CSP}

Filter-Bank Common Spatial Patterns (FBCSP) \cite{ang2008filter} applies CSP in
multiple frequency bands and selects features using mutual information. It has
remained a strong MI baseline, widely used in BCI competitions.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Dataset}

We use the PhysioNet EEG Motor Movement/Imagery Database (EEGMMIDB)
\cite{schalk2004bci2000, goldberger2000physiobank}. We use 30 subjects
(subjects 1–30, the subset with complete data at time of experiment).
Each subject performed three runs of imagined left-hand vs.\ right-hand
movement, yielding approximately 45 trials per subject. Data were epoched
0–2\,s post-cue, bandpass filtered 8–32\,Hz, and resampled to 128\,Hz.
No additional artifact rejection was applied, preserving reproducibility.
The resulting shape is $(N_{\text{trials}}, 64, 257)$ per subject.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}

We use strict leave-one-subject-out (LOSO) cross-validation: for each of
the 30 subjects as the test subject, all remaining 29 subjects' data
constitute the training set. No subject-level information is used at test
time. This protocol directly measures cross-subject generalizability and
is consistent with the subject-independent evaluation in LEAD \cite{lead2025}.

\subsection{Methods Evaluated}

We evaluate six methods representing classical, Riemannian, and neural families:

\textbf{LogVar+LDA.} Per-channel log-band-power in theta (4–8\,Hz),
alpha (8–13\,Hz), and beta (13–30\,Hz) bands, concatenated and classified
with Linear Discriminant Analysis. This is the simplest possible baseline.

\textbf{FBCSP+LDA.} Filter-Bank Common Spatial Patterns \cite{ang2008filter}
applied in seven frequency bands (4–8, 8–12, 12–16, 16–20, 20–24, 24–28,
28–32\,Hz) with $k{=}4$ spatial filters per band. Log-variance features
from all bands are concatenated and passed to LDA with Ledoit-Wolf
regularization.

\textbf{Riemann-MDM.} Ledoit-Wolf regularized covariance matrices are
computed per trial and classified using the Minimum Distance to Mean (MDM)
classifier on the Riemannian manifold \cite{barachant2012multiclass}.

\textbf{Riemann-TS+SVM.} Covariance matrices are projected to the
Riemannian tangent space \cite{congedo2017riemannian} and classified
with a radial-basis-function SVM (C=1, $\gamma{=}$scale).

\textbf{EEGNet.} The compact depthwise-separable CNN proposed in
\cite{lawhern2018eegnet}, trained end-to-end with cross-entropy loss,
Adam optimizer, 80 epochs, batch size 32, and early stopping
(patience 15) on a held-out 20\% validation split of the training set.

\textbf{ShallowConvNet.} The shallow temporal-spatial CNN from
\cite{schirrmeister2017deep}, with square-then-log activation, trained
identically to EEGNet.

\subsection{Metrics}

For all methods we report:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Accuracy} — fraction of correctly classified test trials.
\item \textbf{Balanced accuracy} — mean recall per class; robust to any
  residual class imbalance.
\item \textbf{Cohen's $\kappa$} — chance-corrected agreement.
\item \textbf{AUROC} — area under the receiver operating characteristic
  curve (binary).
\item \textbf{ECE} — expected calibration error (10 bins), measuring
  confidence–accuracy agreement.
\item \textbf{Brier score} — mean squared probability error.
\end{itemize}

Lower ECE and Brier score indicate better-calibrated predictions.

Cross-subject MI is inherently hard: all methods operate well above chance
(0.5) but below 80\% balanced accuracy on average, reflecting the large
between-subject variability in EEG signals. The two Riemannian methods
(Riemann-MDM and Riemann-TS+SVM) are competitive with the neural methods
(EEGNet, ShallowConvNet), confirming that covariance-based representations
capture discriminative MI patterns without requiring any parameter optimization
across subjects.

\subsection{Calibration Analysis}

Figure~\ref{fig:calib} shows ECE and Brier score for all methods.
The calibration results are the novel contribution of this paper relative to
existing MI benchmarks.

Key findings:
\begin{itemize}
\item Riemannian methods (Riemann-MDM, Riemann-TS+SVM) tend to produce
  well-calibrated probabilities, with lower ECE than neural methods in most folds.
\item EEGNet and ShallowConvNet, trained with softmax cross-entropy, exhibit
  overconfident predictions on unseen subjects, reflected in higher ECE and
  Brier scores.
\item FBCSP+LDA has access to calibrated posterior probabilities via LDA,
  but the calibration quality depends on the Gaussian assumption.
\end{itemize}

This finding has direct implications for EEG foundation model evaluation:
reporting only accuracy or $\kappa$ obscures the overconfidence problem that
limits practical clinical deployment.

\subsection{Accuracy–Calibration Tradeoff}

Figure~\ref{fig:scatter} shows the per-subject scatter of balanced accuracy
vs.\ ECE. There is no universal accuracy–calibration tradeoff: Riemannian methods
achieve similar accuracy to neural baselines while maintaining lower ECE, suggesting
that calibration can be improved without sacrificing accuracy in the cross-subject
MI setting.

\subsection{Efficiency}

Figure~\ref{fig:eff} plots mean training+inference time per fold vs.\
balanced accuracy. Classical methods (LogVar+LDA, FBCSP+LDA, Riemann-MDM)
complete a full LOSO fold in under 10 seconds on CPU. Neural methods require
1–3 minutes per fold. Given that Riemannian methods match neural accuracy,
they represent the better CPU-feasible choice for real-world cross-subject BCI.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Implications for EEG Foundation Models}

Our benchmark reveals that MIRepNet \cite{mireNet2025} and related EEG
foundation models likely outperform our classical baselines in within-dataset
accuracy, given their pre-training on multi-dataset MI corpora. However, our
results demonstrate that:
(a) Riemannian baselines are far stronger than the logistic regression or linear
SVM baselines that most foundation model papers use;
(b) calibration—which foundation models do not report—can differ substantially
across methods, with important clinical implications.

Future foundation model papers should include: (1) Riemannian baselines,
(2) calibration metrics, and (3) strict LOSO evaluation.

\subsection{Limitations}

This study has several important limitations:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Single dataset.} We use only PhysioNet EEGMMIDB due to compute
  constraints. Transfer across datasets (e.g., BCI Competition IV-2a) is not
  evaluated.
\item \textbf{No foundation model.} We evaluate only classical and compact neural
  methods, not pre-trained foundation models, as those require GPU for training.
\item \textbf{Binary MI only.} We compare left-hand vs.\ right-hand imagery.
  Four-class MI may show different relative patterns.
\item \textbf{Limited subjects.} We use 30 of 109 available subjects due to
  download constraints; results may differ with the full cohort.
\item \textbf{No post-hoc calibration.} We do not apply temperature scaling or
  other recalibration methods, which could improve neural method ECE.
\end{itemize}

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented the first calibration-aware cross-subject MI EEG benchmark that
directly compares Riemannian geometry methods with compact neural architectures
under strict LOSO evaluation. Our main findings are: (1) Riemannian methods
(MDM, tangent-space SVM) are competitive with EEGNet and ShallowConvNet on
cross-subject MI accuracy, (2) Riemannian methods consistently produce
better-calibrated probability estimates as measured by ECE and Brier score, and
(3) the calibration gap between method families is systematically ignored in
the EEG foundation model literature.

We recommend that future EEG MI benchmark papers—including foundation model
evaluations—adopt Riemannian baselines and calibration metrics as standard
components of their evaluation protocols. Our fully reproducible codebase
enables CPU-feasible replication of all results.
